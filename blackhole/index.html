●  Project brief

Mystery, made legible

An A1 infographic that turns the most extreme objects in the universe into something clear, playful and easy to follow.

01  Topic selection

Why black holes?

They sit where mystery, extreme physics and striking visuals meet: gravity so strong that not even light escapes. Event horizons, spaghettification and singularities simplify into clear, engaging visuals, and ongoing discoveries make the subject instantly captivating.

02  Objective

Explain it, without the overload

Show how black holes form, behave and shape the universe, so viewers grasp the key facts quickly. Concise text, strong visual hierarchy and intuitive illustrations spark curiosity and build scientific literacy without the technical weight.

03  Target audience

Curious minds, big ideas

01School-going kids02Young adults

Fascinated by space, without an advanced science background. A fun, energetic tone and eye-catching visuals keep complex ideas approachable and turn learning into an adventure.

2.1Narration style

Fun, friendly and approachable, without losing accuracy.

A simplified, upbeat voice lets young learners follow along confidently. Playful illustrations and clear visual cues break gravity, event horizons and light bending into bite-sized concepts, blending education with entertainment so a heavy topic becomes a memorable experience.

2.2Visual style

LINE ART +
BRUTALISM

A bold, minimalist style with a raw edge, borrowed from mid-20th-century architecture: honest structure, monochrome palettes, heavy geometric forms, large type, rigid grids and high contrast. Paired with fine line art, it gives the poster an unapologetic simplicity that grabs attention.

03
●  Final design

The Space Donut With No Exit

One A1 poster. One black hole. Everything else orbits it.

The black hole sits at the centre of the poster, with red markers and leader lines tying each part of it to a short, plain-language explanation. The edges answer the big questions: what a black hole is, its types, the Penrose diagram and the theory of relativity, plus a quick fact.

A QR code invites viewers to scan for AR and take the black hole off the page.

  • CentreBlack hole illustration on a star grid
  • Call-outsEvent horizon, singularity, hot spot, jets, cosmic shadow, accretion disk
  • Fact rowPenrose diagram, quick fact, types of black holes, theory of relativity
  • Extras"What is a black hole?" intro and a scan-for-AR QR code

●  Learnings

What stays with me

  1. Simplify, don't dilute

    Turning relativity, singularities and the Penrose diagram into language a school kid can follow took more care than writing it the technical way. Every line had to stay simple and still be true.

  2. Hierarchy is the narrator

    Putting one strong illustration at the centre and letting the call-outs orbit it meant readers could start anywhere and still find their way. The layout does the storytelling.

  3. Restraint makes brutalism readable

    Limiting the poster to black, white and one red let a raw, high-contrast style feel bold instead of noisy. Red became a signal for "look here".

  4. Vector precision, raster motion

    Drawing the disc line by line in Illustrator and finishing it with a spin blur in Photoshop showed me how to layer crisp structure with texture and movement.
